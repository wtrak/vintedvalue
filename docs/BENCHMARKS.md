# Benchmark cases

These are qualitative regression checks based on real sourcing examples. They are not hard-coded outputs; they document the intended behavior.

## 25 USSR lenticular pin badges

- ~25 physical pieces
- visually interesting Soviet sports/cartoon/Olympic themes
- purchase was roughly $12 all-in to Poland
- very light
- expected behavior: **BUY / STRONG BUY**
- likely strategy: many individual $2.50+ auction lots, grouping only weak duplicates

## ~70 Yugoslav pins

- generic-to-moderately-interesting mixed ex-Yugoslav badges
- acquisition around $0.20/piece delivered to Poland
- expected behavior: **BUY**
- important reasoning: weak average rarity is offset by extremely cheap acquisition and negligible freight

## 78 PRL matchboxes containing watch parts

- unusual watchmaker-workshop inventory with collectible period packaging
- asking price around $100
- unknown exact contents
- expected behavior: usually **PASS or aggressive OFFER**
- should not assume 78 individually valuable watch parts

## 400–500 mixed vintage keychains

- asking around $73
- materially heavier than pins
- expected behavior: **BUY/OFFER depending on weight and visible quality**
- tool must account for a large Meest allocation instead of treating freight as negligible

## Cheap vintage beer coasters

- dozens of recognizable brewery coasters
- purchase around $4 total
- expected behavior: **BUY**
- strategy should group by brewery/region rather than force every coaster into an individual auction lot

## General rule

A source lot should not be rejected merely because average individual retail value is low. If acquisition cost is tiny, freight is tiny, and the lot creates many auction opportunities, the expected first-pass contribution can still be attractive.
